Detailed Description

template<class TopologyT, class GeometryT, class NodeDescriptorT, class LocalSolverT, typename LargeVecT = std::vector<double>, typename dof_index_t = unsigned int>
class GlobalLoop::GeneralizedEigenvalue< TopologyT, GeometryT, NodeDescriptorT, LocalSolverT, LargeVecT, dof_index_t >

Generalized eigenvalue problem in the bulk and skeletal unknowns of an HDG method.

This global loop assembles the stiffness matrix \(A\) and the mass matrix \(M\) of the generalized eigenvalue problem \(A x = \lambda M x\), where \(x\) contains the skeletal unknowns and the bulk unknowns. Its typical local solver is LocalSolver::DiffusionEigsWeighted. In contrast to the condensed eigenvalue loops (NonlinearEigenvalue, ShiftedInverseEigenvalue), the bulk unknowns are kept, which makes the problem linear in the eigenvalue and allows for mass on the skeleton (e.g. on hypernodes). Both matrices are symmetric and positive semi-definite. After removal of the Dirichlet unknowns, \(A\) is positive definite if every connected component of the hypergraph contains a Dirichlet face or a hyperedge with positive reaction coefficient; otherwise, the constants are in its kernel. Thus, the problem can be solved by sparse eigenvalue solvers in shift-and-invert mode, e.g. by scipy.sparse.linalg.eigsh, with a shift that is not an eigenvalue (zero if \(A\) is positive definite, negative otherwise).

The unknowns are ordered as follows: The first n_skeleton_dofs() unknowns are the skeletal unknowns (in the order of the hypernode factory), followed by n_loc_bulk_dofs() unknowns for every hyperedge (in the order of the hyperedges). The matrices are returned in coordinate format (entries with the same row and column index have to be summed up), and the Dirichlet unknowns (cf. dirichlet_indices) have to be removed by the user.

Moreover, the global loop provides the values of a vector of unknowns at tensorial points of the hyperedges and of their faces together with the physical coordinates of these points. Together with Gaussian quadrature, this allows to evaluate integrals of discrete functions (e.g. of eigenfunctions) times arbitrary functions, e.g., to compare discrete and exact eigenfunctions.
